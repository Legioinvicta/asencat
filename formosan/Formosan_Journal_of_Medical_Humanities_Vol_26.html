BIOETHICAL CONCERNS OF EMERGING AI TECHNOLOGIES IN CYBER-MEDICINE

Abstract

Innovative biotechnological discoveries in medicine are significantly contributing to the advancement of human health and the overall quality of life. Increasingly, these developments aim to overcome certain natural biological limitations through the enhancement of human capabilities. This is being enabled by the integration of converging technologies and artificial intelligence within the emerging field of cyber-medicine.

However, such progressive aspirations raise substantial bioethical and legal concerns high-lighting the unpredictability of long-term consequences, as well as the potentially uncontrollable side effects that may arise from altering the fundamental nature of human life in a post-humanist context.
Transhumanism, which promotes the radical transformation of human existence, introduce a host of complex anthropological, philosophical, bioethical and legal questions. Central to these concerns are issues related to biological identity, human dignity and the preservation of essential anthropological constants. In this context, it becomes imperative to establish clear algorethical frameworks that can guide the responsible development and deployment of AI-driven technologies, thereby safeguarding core human values.

Key words:

algorethics, Artificial Intelligence (AI), bioethics, cyber medicine, converting technologies, healthcare transformation, law, posthuman identity, transhumanist anthropology.

Introduction

The continuous advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly influencing nearly every aspect of human life. In medicine, it plays a particularly significant role by transforming traditional approaches to diagnosis, treatment planning, and healthcare management through clinical decision support (CDS).[1] These advancements are driven by a range of AI techniques, such as machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), natural language processing (NLP) and recently, large language models (LLMs) used to process and interpret unstructured medical records, generate clinical documentation and assistant support in medical research. More recently, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have significantly improved the accuracy of medical imaging diagnoses, while Natural Language Processing (NLP) algorithms have enhanced the extraction of insights from unstructured data, including Electronic Health Records (EHRs).[2]
Through the application of advanced algorithms and the analysis of large-scale datasets, AI enables faster and more accurate diagnoses, supports personalized therapeutic interventions, and improves the overall efficiency of healthcare professionals. Emerging integrative technologies are fundamentally transforming medical practice, encompassing fields such as cyber-medicine, robotic medicine and digiceuticals innovations that seamlessly merge digital technologies with biomedical sciences to drive the future of healthcare.

Cyber-medicine is an emerging interdisciplinary field combining medicine, AI, and cybernetic technologies to integrate biological and digital systems. It aims to enhance, restore, or surpass human physiological and cognitive functions. Rooted in transhumanist principles, cyber-medicine employs AI-driven diagnostics, bioelectronic implants, neural interfaces, and data-driven innovations to redefine health, disease, life, and mortality, ultimately pursuing human enhancement.
Nonetheless, these advancements bring potential challenges and significant ethical concerns. The integration of AI and emerging technologies in medicine raises critical issues related to patient safety, data privacy, and the possible reduction of the human element in essential decision-making processes. Furthermore, fears of loss of control, technical failures, and unequal access to healthcare underscore the urgent need for responsible and transparent development and implementation of these technologies to ensure they benefit all stakeholders fairly and ethically.

This prompts urgent bioethical, anthropological, and legal questions, especially within the realm of cyber-medicine, a field where AI and digital technologies increasingly blur the boundaries between biological and artificial components, giving rise to novel bio-hybrid systems. Such a despotic digital anthropology reorients the focus away from defining what humanity is toward exploring what humanity could become. This shift further raises critical questions: Are we permitted to breach the natural barriers set by biology in our pursuit of enhanced human capacities? How can we resist the alluring temptation to endlessly improve ourselves and future generations through cybernetic interventions? Where should we draw the line in this rapidly evolving technological landscape? How can we ensure transparency and accountability in AI systems without leaving regulators behind in the race of rapid techno-medical progress? Finally, in the rapidly evolving field of cyber-medicine, can the widening gap between scientific knowledge and technological capabilities keep pace with society’s ethical awareness and understanding of the human values at stake?

Many of these challenges can be effectively mitigated through thoughtful system design, adherence to algorethical programming principles, transparency, and responsible application. Moreover, the establishment of clear regulatory frameworks and guidelines is essential to ensure the safe, ethical, and equitable deployment of AI technologies in medicine.

  • Anthropological Challenges in the Age of Artificial Intelligence: Toward a Posthuman Future
    • Artificial Intelligence is broadly defined as the ability of a digital computer to perform tasks commonly associated with intelligent beings.[3] More precise, “AI system means a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment with the ability to infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments”.[4] It can be broadly classified into three distinct levels: narrow AI (ANI), general AI (AGI) and super intelligent AI (ASI), each representing a progressively more advanced stage of cognitive capability.
      Since, AI is revolutionizing the whole aspects of human life, it is broadly considered to be a determining factor for the future of humanity, as it will substantially transform individual lives and impact on human communities.[5] Due to these concerns, the esteemed Professor Cheng-tek Tai issued a cautionary warning, stating that „AI will change the way we live and pose a danger to disrupt human civilization, traditional values and moral codes“.[6]
      One of the most promising – yet simultaneously concerning – aspects of AI lie in its application within modern, highly sophisticated cyber-medicine. This emerging field enables humans to transcend biological limitations through converging technologies and reconstructive biotechnological enhancements.
      Driven by a supernatural desire to transcend his inherent nature and biological limitations, modern man aspires to become faber sui ipsius/the creator of himself, by transcending his own natural processes, absolutizing the biological dimension and artificially altering the conditions of life. This is pursued through neo/eugenic enhancement of physical, psychological, and intellectual capacities. As the Parliamentary Assembly has noted:
      "The pervasiveness of new technologies and their applications is blurring the boundaries between human and machine, between online and offline activities, between the physical and the virtual world, between the natural and the artificial, and between reality and virtuality. Humankind is increasing its abilities by boosting them with the help of machines, robots and software. A shift has been made from the “treated” human being to the “repaired” human being, and what is now looming on the horizon is the “augmented” human being”.[7]
    • Supporters of human enhancement, transhumanists and posthumanists, believe that technological progress will improve human living conditions by influencing their biological structure, making natural functions more efficient and correcting natural deficiencies that are sources of dissatisfaction and frustration.[8] They advocate the redesign of natural human abilities with improved functions, dual tendencies that would seek to slow down or eliminate aging and greatly increase human intellectual and psychological abilities through scientific research in order to overcome fundamental human limitations. For example, Max More, founder and president of the Extropy Institute, argues that undesirable aspects of human life should not be passively accepted. He promotes the use of science and technology to overcome natural and traditional limits on human potential, including those related to longevity, intelligence, vitality, and freedom, emphasizing the importance of challenging biological constraints rather than resigning to them. A great advocate of improvement techniques is certainly Julian Savulescu. He considers it our duty: “We have a moral obligation or a moral reason to improve ourselves and our children. Indeed, it would be wrong not to do so”.[9] In this vein we can quote the Kevin Warwick's statement: „I was born human. But this was a coincidence of fate - a condition of time and place only. I believe it is something we have the power to change”.[10] Paradoxically, „Those who choose to remain human and refuse to improve will have a serious handicap... They will be a subspecies and become the chimpanzees of the future”.[11]
    • For some, transhumanism is the glorious future of humanity. For others, it heralds a radical change in humanity that will lead us into a posthuman state. A technology entrepreneur, software developer, best known as the co-founder of Microsoft Corporation, Bill Gates has spoken pessimistically about the dangers of artificial intelligence, expressing concern over its potential to pose serious threats to humanity.[12] Technological visionary, Elon Musk, has expressed deep concern regarding artificial intelligence. He views AI as the greatest existential threat facing humankind, “potentially more dangerous than nukes”.[13] A futurist philosopher, Nick Bostrom also argues that AI will pose a threat to humankind. He argues that sufficiently intelligent AI can exhibit convergent behavior such as acquiring resources or protecting itself from being shut down, and it might harm humanity.[14] Renowned astrophysicist Stephen Hawking believes artificial intelligence could spell the end of the human race. Once humans develop superintelligence of AI, it may take off on its own and redesign itself at an everincreasing rate.[15] This raise the basic threat, that Humans, who remain “limited” by biological evolution, could not compete with those enhancement posthumanists and would be superseded by them.
    • The biggest concern represents the ideology of technological singularity, the most extreme and fatal form of transhumanist thought, which through the exponential, uninterrupted acceleration of technology development, enables non biological intelligence to reach and surpass the level of human intelligence.[16]Transhumanist, inventor, and futurist Ray Kurzweil predict that singularity is a future period during which the pace of technological change will be so rapid, its impact so deep, that human life will be irreversibly transformed. He predicts that convergence of humans and machines as an inevitability.
    • Those questions have opened the Pandora’s box and raised concerns, which has been recognized also in the Statement on AI Risk[17] , signed by more than seven hundred AI experts. They are predicting that rapid development of science has already overstepped into the direction to exhibitionist dehumanization of post evolutionary development.
    • Optimistic enthusiasm for biotechnological advancements has spread through modern society like a secularized faith in material progress, promoting the idea that with unrestricted freedom in boundless research, humanity can maximize benefits by enhancing quality of life through biotechnological engineering through convergent replacement and enhancement techniques[18] in contemporary medicine.
    • Human enhancement includes physical/cognitive/emotional improvement, as also a life expectancy extension. Physical enhancements refer to the use of biomedical devices (artificial organs, neuro-chips, implants, etc.). Cognitive enhancement tries to expand the mental memory and intellectual capacities (through cybernetic implants or deep stimulation of the central nervous system). Emotional enhancement is basically oriented toward mood improvement through neuropharmacology or stimulation AL devices. The final aim of eugenic improvement is to expand life trying to achieve the myth of biotechnological immortality.[19]
    • AI’s integration into healthcare promises a paradigm shift towards preventive medicine, emphasizing early detection and intervention in age-related declines. Furthermore, by harnessing the power of predictive analytics, AI can explore vast datasets to uncover biomarkers of aging and offer personalized strategies to slow or even reverse the aging process. This includes leveraging AI for genomic interventions, where it could guide the editing of genes associated with aging mechanisms, enhancing cellular repair, resilience, and longevity.[20]
    • Ray Kurzweil believes that, thanks to artificial intelligence, we will soon be able to “upload” our consciousness into machines in order to achieve biological, bionic, and virtual immortality. He prophetically predicts that between 2045 and 2050, humanity will be compelled to accept hybridization in order to achieve what he calls metensomatosis. It implies the transfer of human consciousness into an informational system after brain death.[21]
    • Those realization of transhumanist ambitions are focused on the use of new NBCI / convergent technologies of the 21st century: nanotechnology (N), biotechnology (B), cognitive (C) sciences and informatics (I) in order to develop synthetic biology, to create nano-devices that will enable the connection of the human nervous system and computer technology. The combination of these technologies confirms the fact that transhumanism is a movement that strives to unify scientific achievements and change the paradigms of life, inventing a new cybernetic fusion of human android.
    • The core idea of cybernetics suggests that a human being can be understood primarily as a form of information—before being viewed as a physical composition of blood, bone, neurons, and synapses.[22] Through the process of cyborgization, the human increasingly transforms into a “technological extension of their own replica”, a remnant of the biological human, becoming a semi-organic entity that survives and functions through technological support.[23] This transformation blurs the lines between what is natural and what is technologically enhanced or altered. In this context, biology becomes technology, and technology becomes biology.[24]
    • When soft or physical robots are machine-like in appearance, they are referred to as mechanoids. Robots designed to mimic the human form are called humanoids, while those crafted to closely resemble human aesthetics are known as androids. Meanwhile, the human being is progressively evolving into homo cybernetics—a being integrated with bionic or robotic implants—giving rise to new anthropological categories such as the transhuman and the posthuman.While transhumanism appears as an improvement on the current state of humanity, posthumanism refers to human with the expanded abilities into the humanoid robots. [25] They could be achieved through advanced AI technologies, which include several progressive approaches, such as machine learning (deep reinforcement learning), machine reasoning (which includes planning, scheduling, knowledge representation and reasoning, search, and optimization), and robotics (through the integration of all techniques into cyber-physical systems).[26]
    • Analyzing the preceding chapter, we were given the opportunity to observe how modern technological progress not only facilitates but also ideologically supports the secularized vision of transhumanism. It becomes evident that such advancements tempt individuals to abandon the innate limitations of the human body, genetic identity, and natural self, seduced by the promise of absolute control over life, its extension, and even its redefinition through innovations in cybermedicine.
  • Progressive AI Technologies in Cybernetic Healthcare: Toward a PostBiological Medicine
    • Artificial intelligence has been explored in medicine since the 1970s, but the advent of digital imaging and the rise of deep learning in the 2010s marked a significant breakthrough. These developments enabled AI systems to achieve expert-level performance in fields such as radiology, pathology, dermatology, and ophthalmology. Today, AI is transforming the healthcare sector by offering innovative, data-driven solutions. It is increasingly integrated into diagnostics, treatment planning, and genomics-based personalized medicine. By processing vast datasets and recognizing complex patterns, AI technologies can even surpass human performance in certain domains. The benefits are manifold: greater diagnostic accuracy, reduced costs, and significant time savings. Furthermore, AI has the potential to revolutionize personalized care, optimize drug dosing, improve population health management, inform clinical guidelines, provide digital medical assistants, support mental health services, enhance patient education, and strengthen the trust between patients and healthcare providers.[27]
      Trustworthy AI technologies and their integration into healthcare and telemedicine have brought numerous significant benefits, including: Increased precision in diagnostic procedures; AI-guided robotic systems for greater precision during operations to perform minute incisions minimizing the risk of complications; A simulations of virtual replicas of digital-twin patients, using data from various sources to model diseases and test therapeutic approaches; 3D bioprinting enabling the development of organic tissues and organs that can save lives and solve the problem of donor shortages; The development of smart e-medicine systems enabling continuous drug absorption; Reliable and safe healthcare delivery systems through advanced endoscopic capsules; Devices for the removal of atherosclerotic plaques in coronary and cerebral arteries; Nano-robots for targeted delivery of cytostatics directly to metastatic sites; Biomimetic nanostructures capable of repairing or replacing DNA and other organelles; Biotechnological advancements in restoring lost organ function; Enhanced algorithmic understanding of diseases and their predictable progression; Epidemiological surveillance, pandemic prevention, and responses to emerging health threats; … and many other transformative innovations in modern medicine.
    • A particularly promising and rapidly advancing field within physical and rehabilitation medicine is bionics. This area focuses on developing technologies that integrate mechanical, electronic and biological systems to restore or enhance human function. Notable innovations include bionic prostheses and robotic orthoses, which are designed to restore mobility in individuals with limb loss or motor impairments. In addition, the field is expanding to encompass a wide range of bionic organs and body parts, including bionic fingers, legs, arms, ears, eyes, tongues, jaws, and hearts, as well as artificial lungs, tracheae, livers, pancreases, bladders, kidneys, hips, skin, tissue, prosthetic limbs, silicone-based speech prostheses, and artificial spinal discs. In parallel, bionic clothing—engineered to work in tandem with mobile exoskeleton systems—is being developed to help individuals with physical disabilities regain independence and mobility in everyday life.[28]
    • Nanotechnology holds the potential to revolutionize medicine by enhancing or repairing neural communication within the brain and improving biocompatibility and integration between brain tissue and cybernetic devices. The rapid progress in recent years has the potential to create effective brain-computer interfaces (BCI), which is being used to help patients suffering from Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, speech disorders, brain trauma and various types of paralysis. In the future, synthetic telepathy using EEG waves could even be developed.[29]
    • A range of neuro-stimulation technologies is already in clinical use to enhance cognitive function and manage neurological disorders. Looking ahead, some projections anticipate broader implementation of radio-frequency identification (RFID) implants, which may eventually influence aspects of human consciousness, emotional states, and physiological responses. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) and vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) are examples of invasive neuromodulation technologies currently being researched for their potential in treating psychological and geriatric neurological disorders. They are also being explored in the management of epilepsy, including seizure monitoring, and in assisting individuals with Locked-in syndrome. These technologies offer promising new avenues for the treatment of complex neurological and psychiatric conditions—such as enabling direct control of robotic limbs, synthetic speech generation, and interventions for treatment-resistant mood disorders or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).[30] Neuro-implants further promise to augment cognitive performance, with proposed applications including the extension of attention span, enhancement of memory and overall increases in intellectual capacity.
    • The convergence of the Internet of Things (IoT), Big Data, and Digital Twin technologies is advancing the development of increasingly realistic and interactive digital avatars. IoT devices enable the continuous collection of data related to an individual’s physical characteristics, movements, and behavioral patterns. This data serves as the foundation for constructing accurate and dynamic digital representations. Big Data plays a central role by enabling the real-time processing and analysis of vast datasets, while artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning (ML), is increasingly employed to extract meaningful patterns from these data streams. ML leverages mathematical algorithms to build predictive models that improve their performance over time through exposure to new data. Within this domain, deep learning (DL) stands out for its use of neural networks that support end-to-end learning directly from raw, multimodal data inputs. [31] Digital Twin technology further enhances this process by creating virtual replicas of individuals, systems, or devices, which can be used for simulation, testing, and development of avatars capable of responding and interacting in a more human like manner. In healthcare, such avatars hold significant promise—ranging from their use in telemedicine consultations and virtual reality therapy, to medical training and education. Moreover, they can be employed to simulate surgical procedures, enabling healthcare professionals to practice and refine their skills in a controlled virtual environment.[32]
    • Another significant achievement of biotechnological integration into medical devices is evident in the field of telemedicine. This is particularly reflected through a diverse range of clinical applications, such as virtual hospitals, which offer numerous benefits including remote access to healthcare services, distant patient observation, continuous patient monitoring, virtual consultations, issuance of electronic prescriptions, cost reduction, increased efficiency, improved patient outcomes, and opportunities for instruction within simulated environments.[33] Complementing telemedicine platforms, wearable AI-enabled devices have become pivotal in enabling continuous personal health monitoring. These devices, equipped with embedded analytics, track vital signs in real-time and utilize advanced algorithms to detect anomalies, thereby providing timely alerts to clinicians and facilitating proactive management of chronic conditions. Those health trackers can monitor heart rate, measure activity, sleep levels, and some have even ECG as a new feature. Digital health tools and well-being applications support self-care and preventive health behaviours.[34] The National Institute of Health (NIH) has created an AiCure App, which monitors the use of medications by the patient via smartphone webcam access and hence reduce nonadherence rates.
    • Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to further enhance existing health information systems, particularly electronic health records (EHRs). Processing systems powered by AI can automate routine and labor-intensive tasks, such as navigating and retrieving information from EHR systems, as well as generating medical documentation and preparing clinical orders.[35] That way, medical professionals can potentially perform a more accurate and detailed analysis of a patient’s complex health data.
    • The integration of artificial intelligence into personalized medicine has the potential to transform therapeutic strategies by enabling the analysis of genetic and molecular data to tailor treatment regimens to individual patients based on their unique characteristics, such as genetics, environment, lifestyle, and biomarkers. This individualized approach aims to improve patient outcomes by analyzing complex datasets, predicting outcomes and optimizing treatment strategies.[36]
      In this domain, the convergence of AI and genotypic data analysis represents a transformative advancement in public health and precision medicine. Systems are being designed to predict the risk of lifestyle diseases, detection of multiple conditions or pathologies, with existing classifiers often trained to only detect a single type of abnormality.[37] On large-scale disease surveillance enables the early detection of emerging health threats, while genomic data provides insights into genetic predispositions, supporting the development of more precise and individualized therapeutic interventions. AI-driven pharmacogenomics can predict individual responses to specific medications, thereby reducing the risk of adverse drug reactions and enhancing therapeutic efficacy.
    • Driven by the desire to overcome biological limitations, the human being—guided by the ideals of transhumanism—seeks to extend the boundaries of the human lifespan. Through the development of cyber-medicine, the body is envisioned as a fusion of the biological and the technological, wherein the integration of artificial organs and the use of cryopreservation and vitrification methods are pursued as pathways toward the achievement of immortality.[38]
      While these developments promise significant benefits in healthcare and beyond, they also provoke growing public concern regarding their ethical, medical and societal implications.
    • Despite their potential, the long-term health and safety impacts of such technologies remain largely unknown, heightening fears associated with the unpredictability. The application of AI in clinical settings introduces complex challenges, including algorithmic bias, data privacy, opaque decision-making processes and questions of accountability. Risks to safety come from a variety of sources, including malicious attacks on software, unethical system design or unintended system failure, loss of human control and the exercise of digital power without responsibility that can lead to tangible harm and problems with the interpretability of "black box" AI models.[39]
    • High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence, set up by the European Commission, in the Ethics Guidelines For Trustworthy AI (2019), consider that Bias can arise in many ways in AI systems.[40] It can lead to: concentration of power in the hands of digital companies, mass surveillance and the strengthening of authoritarianism, misinformation, “echo chambers”, targeted manipulation of citizens and interference in electoral processes and erosion of civil rights.
    • Nuffield Council on Bioethics, in the document, Artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare and research (2018) identified the following ethical issues: the potential for AI to make erroneous decisions; the question of who is responsible when AI is used to support decision-making; difficulties in validating the outputs of AI systems; inherent biases in the data used to train AI systems; ensuring the protection of potentially sensitive data; securing public trust in the development and use of AI technologies; effects on people’s sense of dignity and social isolation in care situations; effects on the roles and skill-requirements of healthcare professionals; and the potential for AI to be used for malicious purposes.[41] A key challenge will be ensuring that AI is developed and used in a way that is transparent and compatible with the public interest, whilst stimulating and driving innovation in the sector.
    • Steering Committee for Human Rights in the Filed of Biomedicine and Health, in the document, The impact of Artificial Intelligence on the doctor-patient relationship (2021)[42] states: The shift of expertise and care responsibilities in clinical care to AI systems can be disruptive, displaced or augmented in many ways in doctor-patient relationship:
      • Inequality in access to high quality healthcare (a risk of creating new health access inequalities)
      • Compromise the transparency to health professionals and patients (patient’s capacity to understand the functionality of AI systems, to make free and informed consent possible)
      • Risk of social bias in AI systems (lead to unequal distribution of outcomes across populations or protected demographic groups).
      • Dilution of the patient’s account of well-being (data representations of patients can come to be seen as an ‘objective’ measure of health and well-being, reducing the importance of contextual factors of health)
      • Risk of automation bias, de-skilling, and displaced liability (clinicians may trust the outputs or recommendations of AI systems not due to proven clinical efficacy, but rather on the basis of their perceived objectivity, accuracy, or complexity.)
      • Impact on the right to privacy (By different forms of dana processing). Countries around the world have introduced laws to protect the privacy of their citizens, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe.
    • For centuries, medical practice has been grounded in the relationship between the healthcare provider and the patient, and it is crucial to ensure that the introduction of AI technologies does not undermine this foundational bond. As noted by critics, “the dominance of a technical-type model can lead to the further depersonalization of the patient and to the elimination of contact between the physician and the patient and, as a result, to the replacement of most doctors with AI systems and robots”.[43]
    • While the integration of artificial intelligence into healthcare brings undeniable benefits in terms of efficiency and precision, it is equally important to consider the humanistic dimension of medicine — a discipline traditionally rooted in empathy, compassion, and interpersonal trust. As technological solutions increasingly mediate clinical interactions, there is a growing need to ensure that these innovations do not erode the core values that define the healing professions. Technology can never substitute the authenticity of human connection nor the moral responsibility inherent in caregiving. Compassion is a cornerstone of quality healthcare and must not be compromised by the use of AI in the alience between the doctor and patient relationship.
    • Rather than viewing AI as inherently incompatible with compassion, some recent frameworks propose that intelligent systems may, under the right conditions, support and even enhance compassionate care. The findings inform a reconceptualization of compassion as a human-AI system of intelligent caring comprising six elements: (1) Awareness of suffering (e.g., pain, distress, risk, disadvantage); (2) Understanding the suffering (significance, context, rights, responsibilities etc.); (3) Connecting with the suffering (e.g., verbal, physical, signs and symbols); (4) Making a judgment about the suffering (the need to act); (5) Responding with an intention to alleviate the suffering; (6) Attention to the effect and outcomes of the response. These elements can operate at an individual (human or machine) and collective systems level (healthcare organizations or systems) as a cyclical system to alleviate different types of suffering. New and novel approaches to human-AI intelligent caring could enrich education, learning, and clinical practice; extend healing spaces and enhance healing relationships.[44]
    • Considering the complex bioethical challenges posed by AI in technological medicine, there is a pressing need for clear bio legal frameworks to ensure that technological advancement remains aligned with core medical virtues and humanistic values.
  • AI Policy Frameworks Regarding Artificial Intelligence
    • There is a growing awareness that a new wave of AI emerging technologies[45] , artificial convergence and robotics are giving rise to complex bioethical and social challenges that may exceed the capacity of current ethical frameworks and regulatory systems to adequately address. One of the major risks associated with emerging medical technologies is the increasing threat of cyberattacks. As artificial intelligence systems become more complex and interconnected, they introduce new vulnerabilities that can be exploited, disrupt medical services and erode trust in digital health systems, making cybersecurity a critical and urgent concern.
    • That has united many organs, such us WHO, UNESCO, UN, Church Magisterium and particular efforts of Council of Europe, to ensure that human rights, democracy and the rule of law are protected and promoted in the digital environment to create a unique ‘ecosystem of trust'.[46]
    • The World Health Organization (WHO) is releasing new guidance on the ethics and governance of large multi-modal models (2024)[47] , a type of fast-growing generative AI technology with applications across health care. To create safe and effective LMMs, WHO underlines the need for engagement of various stakeholders: governments, technology companies, healthcare providers, patients, and civil society, in all stages of development and deployment of such technologies, including their oversight and regulation.
    • At the UNITED NATIONS level, the Centre for Artificial Intelligence and Robotics was established in 2015, with its headquarters in The Hague. The Centre’s work is focused on advancing knowledge in the fields of artificial intelligence, robotics, and other emerging information technologies.
    • UNESCO is preparing the first Global standard-setting instrument on ethics of artificial intelligence based on inclusiveness, trustworthiness and privacy.[48] Nearly all major global powers and regions have developed national or regional artificial intelligence (AI) strategies, recognizing AI’s transformative potential. These strategic frameworks reflect not only the ambition for technological leadership but also the urgent need to ensure that AI evolves in line with ethical principles, human rights, and safety standards. The United States emphasizes strategic leadership through the National AI Initiative and recent Executive Orders[49] focused on safety, fairness, and transparency. China aims to become the global AI leader by 2030, with strong state‑led investments in AI research, infrastructure, and implementation, particularly in surveillance systems and defense technologies — a strategy laid out in China’s New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan.[50] Japan aims for AI-Ready Society through seven social principles of human-centric AI.[51] Canada is focusing on public good, inclusiveness, and research ethics in AI Strategy for the Federal Public Service 2025‑2027.[52] Russia has also launched National Strategy for the Development of Artificial Intelligence until 2030.[53] India emphasizes AI for social inclusion and sustainable development in AI for India 2030 Initiative.[54] Countries in Africa and Latin America are joining the global AI discourse, developing their own national strategies—often with support from international organizations like UNESCO and UNDP—to harness AI for digital transformation and economic growth, while attempting to reduce the global digital divide.
    • Particular attention should be given to the European Union’s regulatory framework on artificial intelligence. The European Assembly has endorsed a comprehensive set of fundamental ethical principles designed to steer the development and deployment of AI technologies. These principles prioritize transparency, justice and fairness, human accountability in decision-making, safety and security, alongside privacy and data protection.[55] Furthermore, the Committee on Artificial Intelligence, established under the authority of the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe, is tasked with overseeing and guiding these efforts.[56]
    • The Steering Committee for Human Rights plays a crucial role in ensuring that AI standard-setting activities remain consistent with human rights norms. These norms are rooted in the European Convention on Human Rights and interpreted by the European Court of Human Rights. As part of its mandate, the Committee is preparing a Handbook on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, expected to be published by 2025. The European Commission has approved the draft guidelines, Commission Guidelines on prohibited artificial intelligence practices established by Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act)[57] , but not yet formally adopted them. These guidelines provide an overview of AI practices that are deemed unacceptable due to their potential risks to European values and fundamental rights.
    • Council of Europe, in the Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (2024)[58] synthesized general obligations to:

      Protection of Human Dignity, Human Rights and Individual Autonomy: AI systems should be designed to uphold and protect personal freedoms and fundamental rights, ensuring that technological progress never compromises the inherent worth of individuals.

      Transparency and Oversight: AI systems must provide clear accessibility and explainability, enabling individuals to understand how decisions are made and how the system operates.

      Equality, Justice, Fairness and Non-discrimination: It is essential that AI systems are developed and trained on diverse and representative datasets to minimize biases. Continuous monitoring and auditing must be established to detect and prevent discriminatory outcomes, especially in sensitive fields like healthcare.

      Privacy, Confidentiality and Personal Data Protection: Strong technical safeguards including encryption, access controls, and cybersecurity protocols must be implemented to protect patient data and ensure confidentiality, with informed consent as a fundamental requirement.

      Accountability, Responsibility, Reliability, Safety and Security: AI systems should be robust, secure against external threats, and designed to avoid unintended harmful actions, following the precautionary principle. Clear accountability mechanisms must be in place to define responsibility among all stakeholders involved.

    • Without clear accountability, patient safety risks increase and trust erodes. Robust frameworks are essential to ensure stakeholders prioritize ethical conduct and patient well-being.[59]
    • The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Recommendation of the Council On Artificial Intelligence[60] identifies five complementary values-based principles for the responsible stewardship of trustworthy AI.[61] It should be lawful, complying with all applicable laws and regulations; ethical, ensuring adherence to ethical principles and values; and it should be robust, both from a technical and social perspective, since, even with good intentions, AI systems can cause unintentional harm.
    • These bioethical documents are complemented by documents of the Church's Magisterium. A document of the Pontifical Academy for Life, Rome Call for AI Ethics (2020)[62] , is among them. The three representatives of the Abrahamic religions—Christianity, Islam, and Judaism, together with representatives of IT companies and the academic sector, signed The Abrahamic Commitment to the Rome Call for AI (2023)[63]. It is also worth quoting the more recent text by the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and the Dicastery for Culture and Education, Antiqua et Nova (2025).[64] They emphasize the importance of an ethical approach to the use of artificial intelligence in medicine, highlighting the need to protect human dignity, rights, and safety in the digital environment. Furthermore, collaboration among religious, technological, and academic communities is called for to ensure the responsible application of AI technologies in line with moral and social values.
    • These prominent advances in AI and its integration into all aspects of human life, have generated considerable scientific interest beyond specialized fields, engaging a wider interdisciplinary community. Over the past five years, numerous private enterprises, research institutions, and public sector organizations have promulgated various sets of principles and guidelines aimed at fostering ethical AI development. An estimated 100 proposals for AI principles have been published in the past decade, and studies have been conducted to identify which principles are most cited.[65]. In mapping and analysis of current principles and guidelines for ethical use of AI, convergence was found on safety, transparency, justice, fairness, non-maleficence and responsibility, while other principles such as privacy, informed consent, solidarity, human dignity and sustainability were underrepresented.[66]. Human dignity and the inherent worth of humans are the central values upon which all other ethical principles rest.
    • In the context of cybermedicine, a primary legal concern is the limited transparency of AI algorithms, often referred to as the "black box" issue, where the internal logic behind decision-making processes remains undisclosed. This lack of transparency poses significant challenges for accountability, patient safety, and trust in AI-driven medical interventions.
    • Ultimately, what unites all these strategies is the shared understanding that AI is not a neutral tool but a powerful socio-technical force. Its development must be accompanied by careful ethical reflection, global cooperation, and clear normative boundaries. It is important for policymakers, organizations, and individuals to stay informed about these laws and regulations to ensure that AI technology is developed and used in a way that respects ethical principles and protects the rights and well-being of individuals, particularly in medicine.
  • AI Policy Frameworks Considering Medicine-Related Documents
    • The rapid advancement of biotechnology in medicine necessitates a corresponding evolution in bioethics and medical deontology to safeguard fundamental human and social values amid this profound technological expansion. As highly sophisticated medical biotechnologies increasingly integrate with human biological material, there is an urgent need for well-defined bio-legal and bioethical frameworks that transcend the current permissive, pluri-perspectivist ethical approaches—often characterized by utilitarian materialism, hedonistic liberalism, and value nihilism.
    • WHO had set the Guidance in the document of, Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health (2021)[67] . The report provides practical advice for implementing the guidance for three sets of stakeholders: AI technology developers, ministries of health and healthcare providers, hoping that these principles will be used as a basis for governments, companies, civil society and inter-governmental organizations to adopt ethical approaches to appropriate use of AI for health, according to main principles:
      • Promoting human well-being and safety and the public interest - The designers of AI technologies should satisfy regulatory requirements for safety, accuracy and efficacy for well-defined use cases or indications.
      • Ensuring transparency, explainability and intelligibility - AI technologies should be intelligible or understandable to developers, medical professionals, patients, users and regulators.
      • Fostering responsibility and accountability – through „human warranty “, which requires application of regulatory principles of the algorithm by establishing points of human supervision.
      • Ensuring inclusiveness and equity - requires that AI should be shared as widely as possible, for health be designed to encourage the widest possible appropriate, equitable use and access, irrespective of age, sex, gender, income, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, ability or other characteristics protected under human rights codes.
      • Promoting AI that is responsive and sustainable - requires that designers, developers and users continuously, systematically and transparently assess AI applications according to the sustainability of health systems, environments and workplaces.
    • Particular aspect of the Council of Europe’s, Artificial intelligence in health care: medical, legal and ethical challenges ahead (2020) [68] , indicates:

      Need for ethical review in biomedical research and limitations on competences and capacities of ethics review bodies to assess the unique risks and opportunities of AI;

      Liability of AI providers in medicine and healthcare;

      Protection of personal data in the context of harmonising data systems and supporting AI innovation and research in Europe.

      Ensuring lawfulness, fairness, purpose specification, proportionality, privacyby-design and default, responsibility, compliance, transparency, data security, and risk management.

      Guaranteeing meaningful control and informed consent for patients and other data subjects.

      Positive obligations for states to protect life and health via national reporting mechanisms.

    • The Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe has also addressed the issue of nanotechnology in Recommendation 2017 – Nanotechnology: balancing benefits and risks for public health and the environment (2013)[69]. The Assembly recommends to develop guidelines on the balance between benefits and risks management methods in order to establish a common standard throughout the world.
    • The Assembly in the document, the brain-computer interface: new rights or new threats to fundamental freedoms? (2020)[70] considers that the following ethical principles must be applied to the development and application of neurotechnology in general and BCI technology in particular:

      Beneficence and prevention of malign use with respect for human rights and dignity.

      Safety and precaution must be ensured before any new applications are put into use.

      Privacy and confidentiality must be protected according to general principles of data protection.

      Capacity and autonomy - this technology should not be used against a subject’s will.

      Human agency and responsibility - must remain the only decision-makers and the primary actors in society.

      Equity, integrity and inclusiveness - it should be implemented with respect for human equality and dignity, including of members of marginalized or vulnerable groups; and it should be made available as widely as possible, especially insofar as they are applied for medical purposes.

      Ensuring public trust through transparency, consultation and education/awareness-raising. With the confidence of the public, in awareness of the benefits as well as the potential dangers.

    • The Steering Committee for Human Rights in the fields of Biomedicine and Health (CDBIO) has published, Report on the application of AI in healthcare and its impact on the doctor-patient relationship (2022)[71] which will include further reflections and recommendations to be considered by the Committee. Ethical standards need to be developed around transparency, bias, confidentiality, and clinical efficacy to protect patient interests in informed consent, equality, privacy, and safety. Such standards could serve as the basis for deployments of AI in healthcare that help rather than hinder the trusting relationship between doctors and patients.
    • Those systems must operate in a manner that is safe, secure, and reliable, with appropriate safeguards in place to mitigate any unforeseen adverse consequences. Ensuring the robustness of AI technologies is therefore a critical priority. In order to, we can no longer rely on basic ethical principalism, since AI introduction in the biomedicine field could possibly violate any fore of them. They need to be redefined according to the sequent Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI: [72]
    • The principle of respect for human autonomy - Humans interacting with AI systems must retain full and effective self-determination, ensuring that the use of AI never deceives individuals or unjustifiably restricts their freedom of choice. AI systems should not unjustifiably subordinate, coerce, deceive, manipulate, condition or herd humans. Instead, they should be designed to augment, complement and empower human skills. Ideally, the informed consent process should be used as a tool to facilitate patient autonomy and self-respect.[73]
    • The principle of prevention of harm - AI systems should neither cause nor exacerbate harm or otherwise adversely affect human beings. AI systems and the environments in which they operate must be safe and secure, ensured that they are not open to malicious use. It implies that AI practitioners should respect the principle of proportionality between means and ends and consider carefully how to balance competing interests and objectives.
    • The principle of justice/fairness - The development, deployment and use of AI systems must be fair. It implies a commitment to ensuring equal and just distribution of both benefits and costs and ensuring that individuals and groups are free from unfair bias, discrimination and stigmatisation.[74] AI systems must avoid reinforcing biases that could disadvantage certain patient groups.
    • The principle of explicability - is crucial for building trust in AI systems. This means that processes need to be transparent, the capabilities and purpose of AI systems openly communicated, and decisions explainable to those directly and indirectly affected.
    • Just as the emergence of bioethics expanded the scope beyond traditional medical ethics, there is now a pressing need for an algorethics that embraces a human-centered vision of technocratic AI bioethics. Consequently, biotechnological and cyber medicine must adopt a critical perspective that clearly defines the boundaries of permissible, rational, and ethical action, regardless of technological possibilities. Such a framework is essential to preserve human integrity and identity, while actively resisting the rise of despotic anthropocentrism.

Conclusion:

--Reframing Technocratic AI Bioethics: Toward a Human-Centric Algorethics

Despite widespread enthusiasm surrounding the integration of artificial intelligence into biomedicine, it is clear that technological advancement cannot be dissociated from ethical reflection and responsibility.
Considering the inherent unpredictability of biotechnological innovations and their profound anthropological and bioethical implications, trust in digital systems must extend beyond mere technical precision. Instead, it must be grounded within a broader epistemological and moral framework that safeguards human autonomy, dignity, and the right to physical and mental integrity.

This imperative calls for the development of a human-centered technocratic AI bioethics, often termed algorethics, that aligns technological innovation with biolegal norms, medical deontology, moral codes, and fundamental human values. In light of the growing influence of transhumanist narratives within cyborg medicine, such a framework must not only guide innovation but also critically resist the reduction of human existence to a project of limitless optimization. It must actively confront the seductive illusions of technocratic well-being, psychophysical perfection, and biotechnological immortality, which risk undermining the fragility, dignity, and moral depth of the human condition.
Navigating the ethical complexity inherent in artificial intelligence and biotechnology demands more than technological innovation—it requires wisdom, responsibility, and a steadfast commitment to the common good. As Stephen Hawking aptly warned, “Our future is a race between the growing power of technology and the wisdom with which we use it.” Only by aligning innovation with the principles of algorethics can we ensure that this race culminates in a future that genuinely benefits humanity. Through proactive engagement, we can harness the full potential of AI to enhance healthcare outcomes while safeguarding our fundamental ethical values.

Medical profession between conscience and professional obligations

Ivana Tucak.Ph.D*. Josip Berdica. Ph.D. ** Gordana Pelčić. M.D.,Ph.D. ***

*Full Professor, Faculty of Law, University of Osijek, Croatia. itucak@pravos.hr

»An individual who breaks a law that conscience tells him is unjust, and who willingly accepts the penalty of imprisonment in order to arouse the conscience of the community over its injustice, is in reality expressing the highest respect for law«. (Martin Luther King, Letter from a Birmingham Jail)

Abstract

Freedom of conscience is today considered a fundamental human right, contained in the most important international human rights documents and in the constitutions of liberal democracies. However, like the vast majority of other fundamental human rights, it is not an absolute category. The real challenge today lies in determining its boundaries. In this paper, the authors address the problem of the contemporary understanding of conscience and its conceptualization, with particular emphasis on the medical profession, which, along with the legal profession, is most closely related to the issues of choice between good and evil, and in fact the values on which conscience is based.

Keywords:

conscience; conscience objection, abortion, professionalism

  • Introduction
    • Freedom of conscience is today considered a fundamental human right, contained in the most important international human rights documents and in the constitutions of liberal democracies. However, like the vast majority of other fundamental human rights, it is not an absolute category. The real challenge today lies in determining its boundaries in Western liberal democracies, which are increasingly marked by ideological pluralism and multireligiosity, as well as the rise of atheistic views among individuals (Martinez-Torrón, 2019: 132–133). Particularly sensitive in this respect is the use of conscientious objection, a right based on freedom of conscience. The question arises of how to reconcile citizens' rights with the rights of other individuals and the interests of society as a whole (Leigh, 2019: 1–2).
    • Legislative interventions in social life are becoming more extensive (Martinez-Torrón, 2019: 132). Historians of medicine point out that the importance of conscientious objection came to prominence only a couple of decades ago, from the 1960s onward, along with the drastic cultural, technological, and medical innovations introduced during that period (Giubilini and others, 2025: 170). During that time, the aforementioned changes enabled individuals to change their sex, receive organ transplants, undergo IVF, and, based on the principle of protecting individual autonomy, abortion on demand, euthanasia, and other procedures were legalised. Most medical services to which physicians now raise conscientious objections therefore emerged relatively recently, having been legalised only in the past few decades (Vargić, 2021: 23)
      In this paper, the authors address the problem of the contemporary understanding of conscience and its conceptualization, with particular emphasis on the medical profession, which, along with the legal profession, is most closely related to the issues of choice between good and evil, and in fact the values on which conscience is based.
  • About commodified conscience
    • Conscience is one of the fundamental phenomena of human existence. It belongs to the very ontological essence of man who, faced with the fear of non-existence, is always concerned about a good existence (Berdica / Nedić, 2019). Acting in accordance with conscience means that we do not act out of fear, coercion or punishment, but out of the feeling that we are doing good, that is, that we are what we are – a being whose existence always wants to be even more authentic. »Man is therefore man«, we read, »insofar as he is a man of conscience«. However, opinion on this issue throughout the history of philosophy and theology has not always been uniform, and this is still evident today in discussions about the place and role of conscientious objection in contemporary liberal constitutional democracies.
    • Aristotle already pointed out in his Politics (1, 2) that the ability to distinguish between good and evil is what distinguishes man from other living beings. Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard wrote in his diary that it is said that every man has a conscience and that this is assumed. Yet, he goes on, there is no virtue that requires such a long and arduous schooling before a man can actually say that he has a conscience. On the other hand, there were those (for example, Nietzsche in La Gaya Scienza, § 355) who considered this distinction to be a kind of inappropriate religious relic, but also those who attached subtle and somewhat complicated interpretations to it. In his work Being and time Heidegger (1985: 305) claims that the »ambiguity of this fact (of conscience) proves that we are dealing here with an original phenomenon of being«. Others will say that »man does not know whether he is and, if so, how unjust he is, unless he exposes himself to a standard outside himself. Man can measure himself by many standards. Some are within him, and some are outside him« (Vučković, 2011: 139). However, it seems that the remark that a society that denies the difference between good and evil is just a form of animal cruelty or a kind of madhouse (Kozelj, 1990: 25).
    • For contemporary discussions on this issue, however, the »spiritual situation of time« (Jaspers, 1998) within which it takes place is critical. Thus, the Italian philosopher Diego Fusaro (2025) points out that today there is an attempt to annul the line of demarcation that in the past, at least in sometimes unstable and only partially visible forms, separated the area of capital from what we generally designated as the »life of the world« (German Lebenswelt). The victory of capital in 1989 marked the increasingly rapid disappearance of the area distinct from it: the immaterial »life of the world« has retreated before the material. This, says Fusaro, fulfilled Marx's announced prophecy from 1847 in his Poverty of Philosophy: the time has finally come when everything has passed into commerce. In a similar way, conscience has been commodified, that is, transformed into a special type of commodity that is now under the influence of capital, power and the market. One could figuratively say that conscience capitulated in front of capital.
  • Conscience and the medical profession
    • However, discussions about conscience are still very current. Especially in the context of public and social action. In the legal profession, there exist potential conflicts between the individual's conscience and the obligations imposed by the profession (Berdica / Nedić, 2019). Likewise, the problem of conscience arises when discussing civil disobedience, where it is necessary to take into account the important differences between these two expressions of opposition to a legal act or obligation (Berdica / Rudan, 2023). Somewhere along that line, in his book The Spirit of the Enlightenment, Tzvetan Todorov (2015: 53) says that in a modern liberal democracy, the individual’s conduct is divided not so much between two realms, the temporal and the spiritual, as between three. Namely, at one end is the private and personal realm: here, the individual is alone responsible, and no one else has any say in these matters. Since the Reformation, freedom and conscience have been expanded to encompass all private acts. At the other end is the legal/rights realm, where the individual is subject to strict norms guaranteed by the state: these he cannot transgress without becoming a criminal. Between the two, says Todorov, is a vast third realm, public or social, steeped in norms and values, which are not, however, binding. »Whereas in the legal realm orders are pronounced and punishments imposed, here advice is given and disapproval expressed in the context of a public debate: this, then, is the realm of moral rules, pressures exerted by fashion, the spirit of the age and religious prescriptions (therefore it is the former realm of spiritual power)«. In this context, it is possible to speak of the internal division of modern man between the private and personal realm that Todorov describes, and the publicly or socially imposed regulations and values for certain professions, including medical ones. To paraphrase the title of one paper, does modern liberal democracy presuppose individual schizophrenia? In some aspects of its functioning, it seems to do so.
    • For one of the most important political philosophers of the last century, John B. Rawls (1999: 323), conscientious objection is »disobedience to a more or less direct judicial or administrative order«. Since the order is directed to us, the authorities know that we are refusing it, because if the authorities were not aware of it, or if we wanted to conceal our refusal, then it would be more of a »conscientious objection avoidance« than a refusal. In the first case, it would therefore be a passive inaction. In contrast, in the second case, a political person is a subject who actively refuses to perform a legally binding act based on their own understanding of the principles of justice. This is an important distinction when considering the level of moral responsibility for passive inaction or active refusal (Berdica, 2024: 157). When it comes to Rawls, it is important to emphasize his understanding of the political conception of the person and his relationship to freedom, because this is the real background of the refusal based on conscientious objection. Only a political citizen rooted in his own understanding of the good in a well-ordered society of constitutional democracy can use this means of respecting his personal understanding of the good, that is, justice (Berdica, 2024: 159).
    • This and similar schematization of conscientious objection in specific professions represent an important contribution to elucidating the importance of conscience itself »as an original phenomenon of being« (Heidegger). The profession that, along with the legal profession, is undoubtedly the most exposed to questions about conscience and its role in performing the profession itself is the medical profession. The increased power over life and health has prompted doctors, as well as lawyers and politicians, to reflect more carefully on the social, legal, and especially moral boundaries within their own professions, as well as on their own responsibility in carrying out their daily tasks. It is evident that a profession that decides on life and health cannot be »liberated« from the question of good and evil, ultimately from the question of virtue. This questions today are increasingly transformed into a problem of justice through the development of bioethics, which is one of the fundamental reasons why moral philosophy and theological ethics have been pushed out of bioethical discussions (Aramini, 2009: 13). However, issues of morality, or more precisely morality and conscience, when performing medical procedures are a permanent feature of everyday medical practice. Therefore, it is not possible to ignore them without neglecting important aspects of the medical profession. In recent decades, as Aramini (2009: 30, 32) point out, we have experienced a kind of return of the category of virtue to discussions, especially in Anglo-American moral philosophy (the socalled ethics of virtue), since it was believed that this category could make a significant contribution to bioethics, as a replacement for the prevalent paradigm of principles or for its integration. Virtue theory has spread among contemporary moralists in place of the abandoned deontological neo-Kantian theory and teleological utilitarian theory. Medical service, as a »practical« activity, is suitable for the application of virtue ethics. »Treatment« and »nurturing« are activities specific to doctors and medical technicians. If a doctor and medical technician »nurturing« and »treat«, this means that they are »good« (virtuous) professionals, since their action achieves a specific goal that is intrinsic to that action. Virtues are those »parts of a doctor's character« that make him good, that help him achieve the goal of his work, and that predispose him to do good and avoid evil.
    • This is neither the time nor the place to discuss a kind of »postmodern understanding of conscience« that would undoubtedly fit into Bauman's Postmodern Ethics (2009). What is important to emphasize, however, is that we live in a time when there are no universal patterns of decision-making. Therefore, when it comes to conscience, we can say that everyone is individually responsible for their own decisions. Doctors decide in accordance with their virtues and understanding of what they should do and what they should avoid.
  • The right to conscientious objection in the Republic of Croatia
    • The Croatian Constitution, adopted in 1990 after the fall of communism, does not explicitly guarantee the right of healthcare professionals to conscientious objection on religious or moral grounds; it is mentioned explicitly only in the context of military service (Art. 47 para. 2). Nevertheless, it is inherently present in the constitutional provisions relating to the protection of citizens’ freedom of conscience and the free expression of religion and other beliefs (Art. 17 para. 3; Art. 40; Horvat Vuković, 2021:452). Conscientious objection is part of the constitutional identity of the Republic of Croatia, reflecting its belonging to liberal democracies that nurture pluralism (Horvat Vuković, 2021:452).
    • Liberal democracies differ from authoritarian forms of government precisely in their tolerance of diverse secular and religious beliefs. However, it is also important to emphasize that a necessary condition is for these protected beliefs to be reasonable and not to imply any form of intolerance (Giubilini and others, 2025: 118). A critical question regarding religious tolerance is the extent to which secular liberal democracies will recognize conscientious objections based on faith (Greenawalt, 2013:91).
    • All laws are based on ethical values, although this may be more or less visible (MartinezTorrón, 2019:135). Laws are never neutral. In liberal democracies where the rule of one person, one vote prevails, these are the values held by the majority of the population. Conflicts between conscience and legal duties thus arise among members of minority communities (MartinezTorrón, 2019:151). Advocates of the right to conscientious objection see the most significant danger precisely in this. If absolute adherence to these so-called neutral laws is demanded, while in reality these are laws supported by the majority, this may result not only in discrimination against individual citizens but also in the violation of the group rights of members of minority religious communities (Martinez-Torrón, 2019:151).
    • In this respect, the principle of proportionality emerges as a fairer solution (Martínez-Torrón, 2019:135; Horvat Vuković, 2021: 460). Thus, the Croatian Constitution provides for the possibility of restricting freedoms and rights “proportionately to the nature of the need for restriction in each case” (Art. 16 para. 2). Restrictions may be prescribed by law for the protection of the rights of others, the legal order, public morals, and health (Art. 16 para. 1).
      In Croatia, the right to conscientious objection is explicitly recognized in positive laws relating to various categories of healthcare workers. Conscientious objection was first introduced by the Medical Profession Act in 2003 (Tucak/Berdica/Seleš, 2025:90). This Act (Official Gazette – 121/03 and 117/08, Art. 20) stipulates that a physician has the right to invoke conscience objection based on their religious, ethical, or moral beliefs. However, this right must not contradict professional rules and should not result in permanent consequences for the patient’s health or endanger the patient’s life. The physician must inform the patient in a timely manner of their conscientious objection and is obliged in such cases to refer the patient to another physician of the same specialty. The physician must also inform their superior about the use of conscientious objection. The obligation of physicians who invoke conscientious objection to refer their patients to another physician is considered highly controversial by some authors. For example, in the case of abortion, this provision forces a physician who invokes conscientious objection, the authors argue, to assist in the commission of an act that they see as an unjustified killing of a fetus, which they view as a rights-holder (Ben-Moshe, 2019: 409). For the physician in such a position, this represents participation in an act they feel morally obliged not to commit (Ben-Moshe, 2019: 409).
    • Authors who consider the duty to refer justified argue that its violation should result in disciplinary proceedings within the healthcare institution where the physician is employed and before the Medical Chamber. Moreover, when it comes to abortion, they argue that such a failure by the physician to fulfil their legal obligation constitutes direct gender discrimination (Horvat Vuković, 2021: 454). It is interesting to note that in the United States, physicians who invoke conscientious objection are not obligated to refer their patients to a specialist of the same field (Giubilini and others, 2025: 10).
    • The question of who should be included in the possibility of invoking conscientious objection when performing specific medical procedures, such as abortion, remains a subject of debate. In Croatia, conscientious objection is also included in the Nursing Act (Official Gazette - 121/03, 117/08, 57/11, 123/ 24; Art. 3 para. 4). The Medically Assisted Fertilization Act grants the right both to healthcare and non-healthcare workers (Official Gazette - 86/12; Art. 44). It is also found in the Midwives’ Code of Ethics (Art. 3. 20).
      In Europe, the issue of conscientious objection among healthcare professionals falls under the jurisdiction of the member states rather than the European Union (Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (2000/C 364/01), Article 10 para. 2), so practices vary across European countries. For example, Sweden, Finland, and Iceland do not provide for conscientious objection in their abortion laws (Giubilini and others, 2025: 17; Marques-Pereira, 2023: 18).
  • The use of conscientious objection regarding abortion
    • In the Republic of Croatia, the most problematic use of conscientious objection by physicians concerns abortion. Countries around the world began legalising abortion one after another in the 1970s, and along with abortion legalisation, they started recognising physicians’ right to conscientious objection, which until then had been used almost exclusively in the context of military service. For example, the United Kingdom introduced conscientious objection in 1967, and France in 1975 (Vargić, 2021: 21–22). The law currently in force in the Republic of Croatia regarding abortion will soon be fifty years old. The Act on Health Measures for Exercising the Right to Free Decision-Making on Childbirth grants women in Croatia the right to undergo an abortion up to 10 weeks from conception (Official Gazette - 18/1978, 88/2009; Art. 15 para. 2; hereinafter: the Abortion Act). Although in Croatia the issue of abortion is at the center of political, religious, and cultural conflicts, the number of abortions performed at the request of pregnant women is in continual decline. In 2024, 8.8 abortions were performed per 100 births, while the year before, in 2023, the number was slightly higher—9.3. The average rate of abortions on request is 358.4 per 100,000 women of reproductive age. By age group, the largest share of women seeking this medical service is among those aged 30 to 39 (43.0%), followed by those aged 20 to 29 (39.7%) (Croatian Institute of Public Health, 2025: 2-3).
    • The Croatian Abortion Act of 1978, adopted during the communist regime, did not allow physicians the right to conscientious objection. This right was recognized only in 2003, as already noted, by the Medical Profession Act. Today in Croatia, nearly 50 per cent of gynaecologists and gynaecologists-in-training invoke conscientious objection regarding abortion (Dnevnik.hr, 2025).
    • When a large number of gynecologists invoke conscientious objection to providing abortion services, abortion laws become ineffective (Giubilini and others, 2025:10). Croatia, therefore, faces a serious problem in ensuring women’s access to lawful, safe, and timely abortion. The use of conscientious objection by physicians calls into question the purpose of the current Abortion Act, as well as the values on which that Act is based. This situation aligns with one of the criteria identified by Kate Greasley, which suggests that the limits on conscientious objection are inadequately defined. The other two criteria outlined by Greasley concern the extent to which the objector is involved in the activity they oppose, and the question of who bears the burden—the individual invoking conscientious objection or those who must take on the duties of the person who objected (Greasley, 2017: 251). Conscientious objection thus calls into question the effectiveness of abortion provisions (Greasley, 2017:253).
    • The UN Human Rights Committee, in its Concluding Observations on the Fourth Periodic Report of the Republic of Croatia on the implementation of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, noted this Croatian problem (The Ombudsperson for Gender Equality, 2025: 247) and called on the authorities to “(t)ake all measures necessary to ensure that the exercise of conscientious objection does not impede effective and equal access for women and girls to confidential, legal and safe abortion services and post-abortion care” (CCPR/C/HRV/CO/4: Concluding observations on the fourth periodic report of Croatia, 26.a). Furthermore, in its 2017 decision, the Constitutional Court of the Republic of Croatia pointed out the need to “modernize” the Abortion Act. It emphasized the duty of the Croatian Parliament to regulate the issue of physicians’ conscientious objection in the new act (Constitutional Court of the Republic of Croatia, 2017, para. 50). Parliament was instructed to do so within two years. Still, it has not fulfilled this obligation to this day.
    • Finally, it is essential to highlight that conscientious objection is an individual act; a legal entity cannot hold this right (Horvat Vuković, 2021:440). If the management of a healthcare institution instructs employees not to perform abortions, this constitutes a clear abuse of conscientious objection (Marques-Pereira, 2023:18).
  • Professionalism
    • The conflict between supporters and opponents of conscientious objection in medicine is ongoing. Opponents do not view conscientious objection as a human right but rather as an unjustified refusal to provide a medical service, which contradicts the principles of professionalism and relevant legal regulations (Giubilini and others, 2025; Vargić, 2021: 19), or as an unwarranted privilege (Leigh, 2019:3).
    • Supporters of the right to conscientious objection in performing abortions at the request of a pregnant woman without medical indications argue that such a procedure is not a “necessary part of the medical profession” (Vargić, 2021: 19; Giubilini and others, 2025: 77). In this section of the article, we focus specifically on the question of professionalism. A medical professional’s refusal to perform the requested activity may be understood as a rejection of their professional responsibility toward patients (Marques-Pereira, 2023:19).
    • One argument against physicians' right to conscientious objection is that they hold a “societal monopoly” over the provision of healthcare services (Giubilini and others, 2025: 3). A. Giubilini and others (2025) present two claims that together form the central thesis of their book: healthcare professionals have neither the responsibility nor the authority to assess whether a particular legal provision is ethically wrong, nor to act on their own ethical judgments that diverge from current professional standards (Giubilini and others, 2025: 4). One fundamental characteristic of professionalism is that individuals voluntarily enter a given profession. In the healthcare profession, physicians customarily take an oath in public ceremonies, declaring that the health of their patients—not their own interests—will be their priority (Giubilini and others, 2025:50). Practicing medicine is grounded in professionalism and in dedication to the best interests of patients (Giubilini and others, 2025:34). In this context, conscientious objection is seen as an obstacle to realizing the right “to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health” (International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, Art. 12.1; Vargić, 2021: 33-34).
    • When discussing professionalism as a reason for rejecting the possibility of invoking conscientious objection in the case of abortion, it is important to consider how ethical and professional guidelines regulate this issue. The Ethics and Professionalism Guidelines for Obstetrics and Gynaecology, issued by the International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics, clearly emphasise that a physician’s conscientious objection is secondary to their professional responsibilities (FIGO, 2021, Recommendations 1; Giubilini and others, 2025:16). However, the Guidelines also state that those who invoke conscientious objection should not suffer discrimination because of it:
      “Obstetrician-gynecologists have a right to be respected for their conscientious convictions, when these are genuine and clearly expressed. Politically motivated or financially motivated assertions of ‘conscience’ are egregiously unethical. Obstetrician-gynaecologists who express conscience-based convictions that are genuine should not suffer discrimination on the basis of their convictions, provided that the obstetrician-gynaecologist fulfils the professional responsibility to see to it that the patient receives the clinical management that the patient has authorised“ (FIGO, 2021, Recommendation, 4).
    • It is also emphasized that the “professional responsibility to refer patients” does not constitute participation in the performance of an abortion (FIGO, 2021, Recommendation 5).
  • Conclusion
    • In the first part of the paper, the authors problematize the concept of conscience, with particular emphasis on the crisis in contemporary discourse on this fundamental problem of human existence. To what extent is discourse on values and the concept of conscience based on them, even possible in a society of universal commodification? The second step attempted to bring conscience closer to the profession that, along with the legal profession, certainly has the most "to do" with conscience - the medical profession. There are many questions when attempting to connect the concept of conscience (and the broader discourse on values) with the medical profession.
    • In the subsequent sections of the paper, we aimed to demonstrate how physicians’ conscientious objection operates in practice, focusing specifically on conscientious objection to performing abortions. We strongly believe that medicine should not discriminate against anyone's personal values (cf. Savulescu, 2006: 297). Additionally, we think that laws and professional medical codes should include mechanisms to prevent the abuse of this institution (cf. Borovečki/Babić-Bosanac, 2017: 320).

Respect for Autonomy in ALS:
Decision-Support, Communication, and Justice as Enabling Conditions

Yuichi Minemura. Ph.D. 峯 村 優 一

Gunma Paz University. Japan 日 本 群 馬 大 學 醫 療 技 術 部 准 教 授

Abstract

This article analyzes the principle of respect for autonomy, often treated as central within the four-principles framework in contemporary bioethics. Rather than reaffirming autonomy as a slogan, I clarify its conceptual content and practical limits by identifying the conditions under which a choice is genuinely autonomous—especially understanding, voluntariness, and freedom from undue influence—and by explaining when clinicians may be permitted or required to intervene to prevent coercion or to support decision-making. I then apply these criteria to patients with intractable diseases, focusing on ALS, where progressive disability and dependence can make autonomous decisions difficult to express, reaffirm, and sustain over time. I argue that autonomy-respecting care in ALS requires more than non-interference: it demands capacity-sensitive consent practices, communication assistance, and deliberative responses to requests to die that address remediable suffering, isolation, and social pressure without collapsing into unreflective paternalism. Finally, I contend that justice is best understood as an enabling condition for meaningful autonomy. Without equitable access to information, assistive technologies, home-care support, respite services, and protection against stigma and discrimination, “respect for autonomy” risks becoming merely formal rather than substantively responsive to patients’ values and vulnerability.

Keywords:

Respect for autonomy; autonomy; reason; amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS); justice

(*This article substantially revises and expands themes discussed in Chapter 5 of The Minds of Patients with an Intractable Disease: From the Perspective of Clinical Psychology and Bioethics, developing a more detailed account of respect for autonomy in clinical contexts*)

In this article, I analyze the principle of respect for autonomy, which in contemporary bioethics is often treated as central within the four-principles framework. My aim is not simply to reaffirm autonomy as a slogan, but to clarify its conceptual content and practical limits: what conditions make a choice genuinely autonomous, and when clinicians may be permitted—or even required—to intervene (for example, to prevent coercion, to address undue influence, or to support decision-making). In doing so, I also note the Kantian moral background of the principle, while focusing primarily on its clinical interpretation through criteria such as understanding, voluntariness, and authorization.

After clarifying the nature of respect for autonomy in this sense, I examine ethical issues faced by patients with intractable diseases—especially ALS—whose progressive disability and dependence can make autonomous decision-making difficult to express, reaffirm, and sustain over time. I argue that autonomy-respecting care in such contexts requires not only non-interference but also practical supports (including capacity-sensitive consent practices, communication assistance, and deliberative responses to a request to die).

Finally, I contend that the value of autonomy cannot be adequately protected without attention to justice understood as an enabling condition rather than a separate, full-scale topic: in clinical settings, autonomy is meaningfully exercised only where patients can access relevant information and services, communicate their preferences, and live free from prejudice and structural disadvantage. These justice-relevant conditions determine whether ‘respect’ for autonomy is substantive rather than merely formal.

  • Autonomy and Reason
    • Autonomy comes from the Greek words autos (self) and nomos (rule). The original meaning of autonomy is "giving oneself rules and acting in a disciplined manner." A person with autonomy can make a decision and act with her own will. In contrast, a person without autonomy, who has lost it or is in the process of losing it, is manipulated by the will of others and acts under that manipulation. The person under manipulation cannot act according to her own will.
    • To summarize the nature of an autonomous person in an easy-to-understand way, we can say that she can (a) make intentional choices, (b) understand the situation, and (c) act without being influenced by dominant forces . If a person cannot decide and act with her own will, she cannot act autonomously. “Making intentional choices” is an essential element of becoming an autonomous person. A person who does not make intentional choices will not be autonomous. “Understanding the situation” does not indicate a person must know everything to act. Understanding the situation denotes the person must understand where she is located, and she can obtain the necessary information and make a judgment to make a deliberate choice and act.
    • “Without being subject to controlling influence” does not indicate there is no controlling influence from the other and the organization. We cannot be unrestricted in all aspects of our social lives. We choose to be bound to a certain extent by the influence of other people and organizations. We live under the influence of what has been protected in society, including many rules, customs, culture, etc., while preserving our autonomy . For example, we must act according to a university's rules while we are university students. When working as a member of society and belonging to an organization, we must act under the organization's rules . In the case of ceremonial occasions or religious ceremonies, we must protect customs and strive to be socially disciplined . The rules of an organization and social customs are a type of authority. We often act under authority. Acting under the authority does not indicate that we lose our autonomy. If we follow these rules and conventions and are under the authority of our will, our decisions and actions are still autonomous.
    • Two clarifications are important here. First, autonomy is often analyzed not as a global “all-ornothing” status but as decision-relative and domain-specific. In other words, a patient’s capacity for autonomy may be preserved for straightforward or low-stakes choices (e.g., deciding where to receive care) while being significantly diminished for more cognitively demanding or high stakes decisions (e.g., agreeing to complex experimental interventions). Second, clinical respect for autonomy is deeply connected to informed consent, where adequate disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness, and authorization function as practical criteria. These points matter especially in progressive neurological diseases, where the patient’s comprehension, communication, and voluntariness may be threatened not only by pathology but also by institutional and social pressures.
    • Moreover, contemporary debates distinguish autonomy from mere preference-satisfaction. Even when a patient can express a preference, the preference may be produced under coercion, misinformation, internalized stigma, or depressive distortion. Thus, “respecting autonomy” can require clinicians to ask whether the patient’s expressed choice is sufficiently reflective, stable, and supported, rather than simply ‘spoken’.
    • The principle of respect for autonomy is based on the idea of the German philosopher Immanuel Kant. Kant constructed a moral philosophy emphasizing the rationality of a human being. According to Kant, a human being has an unconditionally valuable property, a ‘reason’ that other animals do not possess to be a moral being. One must not violate reason under any circumstances. When a rational human being harms herself and commits suicide, or when she ignores the will of others and uses them for her benefit, that type of act would violate human reason. Kant believed that acts that would not respect the autonomy of a rational human being and would use her should never be allowed. According to Kant, one should not use a rational human being as an instrumental ‘means’ as if she treats it as an object. A rational human being must be respected and treated as a noble ‘end’ with ultimate value . The principle of respect for autonomy is established based on Kant's idea that others should not restrict or infringe on the thoughts and actions a rational human being autonomously possesses. This principle is one of the four ethical principles and is the most highly valued principle in modern times, emphasizing autonomy.
    • These clarifications show that respecting autonomy in clinical practice does not mean merely refraining from interference. Rather, it requires clinicians to assess whether a patient’s choice is sufficiently informed, voluntary, and reflective, and—when these conditions are threatened—to provide decision-support and to remove undue influences. This point becomes especially salient in the context of intractable diseases such as ALS, where a patient may retain rational capacities while progressively losing the practical ability to communicate, sustain, or protect her own decision-making. /li>
  • Autonomy and Patients Receiving Treatment for Intractable Diseases
    • In this section, I examine the principle of respect for autonomy by applying the conceptual criteria developed above to patients receiving treatment for intractable diseases, especially ALS. The central ethical question here is not whether a patient’s expressed preference should be accepted simply because it is expressed, but whether the conditions that make a choice genuinely autonomous—adequate understanding, voluntariness, and freedom from controlling influence—are secured and sustained in the patient’s actual clinical and social circumstances. In ALS, progressive disability and dependence can place patients under subtle but powerful pressures (including fear of becoming a burden, social isolation, and institutional constraints), and can also make it difficult for them to communicate or reaffirm their decisions over time. For that reason, clinical respect for autonomy should be understood as an obligation not only to avoid coercion, but also to support the patient’s ability to decide and to express her will through appropriate information, communication assistance, and an environment that protects her from prejudice and unreasonable pressure. This analysis provides the basis for interpreting difficult cases, including a request to die, without collapsing respect for autonomy into either simple compliance or unreflective paternalism.
    • To make this application concrete, I begin with cases of advanced ALS in which patients survive through Tracheostomy Positive Pressure Ventilation (TPPV) and related life-sustaining support. These cases are ethically difficult not because the patient necessarily lacks rational capacities, but because the conditions for autonomous decision-making and its reliable expression can be undermined by progressive dependency, communicative limitation, and social or institutional pressure. Accordingly, this section aims to identify what respecting autonomy requires in such circumstances—namely, careful assessment of decision-making capacity and voluntariness, active protection against coercion or undue influence, and practical supports that enable patients to understand options and communicate their preferences over time.
    • An ALS patient, especially one whose condition has progressed to the point where only a few of her body functions remain or who is in the state of locked-in syndrome, can survive with TPPV to maintain circulatory and respiratory functions. However, an ALS patient with this condition is unable to move her body of her own will and is in a state of extreme disability. A physician can maintain the patient’s consciousness and mental function without dulling, weakening, or stopping. Nevertheless, the patient cannot move her body of her own will and, thus, cannot survive her daily life without the help of her family or supporters.
    • An ALS patient needs support using communication tools so her family and supporters can understand her will via them. When an ALS patient is in a state of locked-in syndrome, she cannot convey her will, even with the use of communication tools. As a result, the patient despairs over her severe condition and has a desire to die. If a physician respects the wish to die of an ALS patient, it indicates that it would allow the patient to commit suicide . However, it would be difficult for a physician to enable an ALS patient who is still conscious, mentally functional, and rational to commit suicide. Medical professionals, the patient's family, and supporters need to understand the feelings of despair experienced by a patient with advanced ALS. It is essential to support the patient as well so that she has the potential to live with hope as long as she retains the ability to live rationally. When the patient has the intention to end her life, medical professionals should not merely respect her wishes. Medical professionals should work with the patient's family and supporters to support the patient in reframing her situation and identifying values-consistent reasons to continue living.
    • Respecting patient autonomy, rather than reverting to paternalistic medicine in which physicians unilaterally decide treatment, is especially important in intractable disease contexts. Yet patients with intractable diseases may face indifference, prejudice, and discrimination, and some may also develop neurodegenerative changes that affect behavior and judgment. For these reasons, clinicians should not equate “respect for autonomy” with mere non-interference; they have responsibilities to provide appropriate information, to support decision-making and communication, and to help secure an environment in which patients can understand their condition and express their preferences without undue pressure or stigma
  • Autonomy, Vulnerability, and the Social Conditions of Choice
    • It is important to recognize that loss of physical motor function does not by itself undermine autonomy. The central ethical challenge is that ALS can progressively impair communication, thereby damaging the patient’s ability to demonstrate understanding and appreciation, even when those mental abilities remain intact. For this reason, clinicians should utilize alternative communication and assistive technologies as autonomy-enabling supports to compensate for an ALS patient's reduced ability to demonstrate understanding and appreciation . An ALS patient fails to communicate a choice because of physical limitations rather than cognitive deficits. An ALS patient has the capacity to understand relevant information and appreciate her own situation. Thus, by distinguishing “cannot communicate easily” from “lacks the capacity to decide”, clinicians must understand that an ALS patient losing the capacity to communicate retains the capacity to understand/appreciate and decide autonomously . These communicative barriers also shape how clinicians should interpret and respond to an ALS patient’s request to die.
    • If an ALS patient’s autonomous request to die is driven by remediable suffering or social isolation, then simply invoking her autonomy risks abandoning the patient. Autonomy-respecting care, in such a case, should be endorsed through deliberation rather than mere permission . In addition, because ALS is progressive, respect for autonomy should include serious engagement with advance care planning (ACP) and advance directives, including preferences about ventilation, gastrostomy, and thresholds for burdensome interventions. Importantly, ACP connects present autonomy with future vulnerability: it is a way for a patient to project her value into the future, while also leaving room for revision as circumstances change . Yet such support and planning are feasible only under just social and institutional conditions.
    • Bioethical discussions often treat justice as a separate principle, but in intractable disease contexts, I argue justice is best understood as a precondition for meaningful autonomy. A patient cannot ‘choose’ among options that are not actually available: without equitable access to home care support, respite services, palliative care expertise, etc. Justice also concerns the moral status of caregivers and supporters. When families are expected to provide near-total care without structural support, caregiver exhaustion, moral distress, and even abuse become more likely, not because caregivers are bad people, but because the social system has set them up for failure. Justice, therefore, requires designing institutions that distribute burdens fairly and protect both patients and caregivers.
    • Finally, justice demands active resistance to stigma and discrimination. If prejudice shapes the patient’s self-conception—making her see herself as “less worthy” or “a burden”—then what appears as an autonomous request may be partly the product of unjust social meanings. A relational conception of autonomy makes this visible: autonomy is exercised within social relations, not outside them, in a fair condition without stigma and discrimination .
    • In sum, the principle of respect for autonomy does not impose an unconditional requirement to comply with any expressed wish. Rather, it requires clinicians and society to secure the conditions under which patients—especially those with intractable diseases—can decide and communicate in ways that reflect their values and understanding. In ALS, this means prioritizing autonomy-enabling supports (communication tools, capacity-sensitive consent practices, deliberative support) and recognizing that justice is structurally intertwined with autonomy.

The Concept of Autonomy amongst Muslim Patients in Malaysia

Anisah Che Ngah Ph.D*. Ammar Hashim M.D**

*(Formerly) Associate Professor. National University of Malaysia. Malaysia
( anisahamidah@gmail.com)

**Head. Risk Management Unit. Sarawak General Hospital. Malaysia.
( ammar.hashim@moh.gov.my)

Abstract

Western medical ethics places autonomy as a material principle in the discipline of medicine that ought to shape the patient-doctor relationship in modern day to day practices. This principle loosely defined as ‘self-governing’, serve as a tool that enhances and promotes the patient’s own capabilities to act according to one’s preferences free from any undesired control and limitation. What is significant in the Western medical ethics is the right of a legally competent individual to make his choice, whether he wants to undergo or withdraw from treatment. Although the Malaysian Court had recognised this right, the Muslim community prefers to follow the commandments in the Holy Qur’an and the Sunnah when making critical medical decisions. Hence the focus of this article is to clarify the Islamic stance on the concept of Autonomy and multiple factors which the Muslim patients take into consideration when exercising their rights. Findings suggest that in Islamic bioethics, autonomy is not absolute, instead religious and cultural values must be taken into consideration.

autonomy, Muslim patients, public interest, limitation

Malaysia consists of three main races, the Malay Muslims being the majority alongside Chinese and Indian Community (Syaza Shukri, 2023). Even though Syariah law provides regulations in all aspects, the Syariah law that is applicable in Malaysia is limited to only personal matters, amongst others, the law on marriage and divorce and the distribution of estates before and after death (Mohd Naim et al, 2023). Details of the subject matter can be found in the Federal Constitution of Malaysia, second List, schedule 9 (Article 74, 77).

This concept of self-autonomy bestowed upon them the authority to make decision on their illness without undue influence from their doctors and further substantiating patients' participation in their health management with decentralization of doctor’s role to act as an advisor rather than behaving as the major decision maker. Despite the widely publicised and acceptance of the over-liberal definition of patient self-autonomy, it does not come without any weak points. The self-autonomy idea has also been generally exploited in the aspect of refusal of treatment regardless of patient’s illogical, non scientific, and incoherent reasoning behind it and the likelihood of repercussions it perpetrated (Catherine M.et al, 2017).

The testimony made in the Qur’an and the Sunnah clearly stated that human beings are attributed with the free will to choose either they want to submit themselves as an obedient servant of God or to follow the path of non-believers. Qur’an is the Holy book for Muslims comprising of basic commands from Allah SWT, whereas Sunnah is the tradition of Prophet Muhammad (Peace be upon him (PBUH), which supplements the commandments from the Holy Qur’an. For a Muslim, there are certain rules and regulations as stipulated in the Qur’an and the Sunnah with instructions as how one should conduct himself. These divine rules function as universal guidance which cover all essential aspects of life , including morally right and appropriate behaviours. Muslims believe that every single creation in the universe which include our complex anatomical structure are attributed to Allah as the supreme creator and will ultimately be returned to him when the time comes, Qur'an, Al-Imran, 3:109.
      To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and to [all] matters be returned. Allah will [all] matters be returned.

  • The application of maqasid syariah in healthcare
    • Syariah is a way of life in Islam and its objective are summarized as the Maqasid Syariah ( Darihan Mubarak e.al., 2022). The application of Maqasid Syariah in medicine was introduced to guide Islamic doctors dealing with medical dilemmas (Aasim I Padela, 2018). This concept can be referred to as ethical framework and practical guidelines for Muslim doctors based on the principles of preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, dignity, and property (MY Rathor, 2011). “Maqasid” is an Arabic word which means heading towards a direction, objective, or purpose. The term “Shariah” literally means spring water extracted from its source, or a clear and straight path derived from a ruling sanctioned by God. Islamic scholar, Shaykh Muhammad Al Tahir Ibn Ashur states that “Maqasid Syariah” is the core knowledge that needs to be learnt by Muslim doctors in interpreting the Islamic texts (Qur’an and Sunnah) with the intention to find clarification for the existing contemporary problems (2013). The principles expounded in Maqasid Syariah offers ethical, moral guidance and wisdom in a process of logical reasoning that complies with God’s commandment to ensure community welfare and general prevention of harm.
  • The concept of autonomy in islam
    • In Islam, the concept of autonomy is entrenched in individual accountability and the freedom to choose, primarily based on the principle of "no compulsion in religion". This is derived from the Holy Qur’an, Al-Baqarah, 2: 256
      Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood… In other words, a competent human being has the religious freedom to make his choice since faith is a personal matter and that individual is given the free will to accept or reject faith. Hence, once the person subscribes to Islam, then the perception of autonomy will be guided by what is stated in the Holy Qu’ran and Sunnah.
      • Preservation of Religion/Faith
      • Islam through the objectives of Maqasid Syariah emphasises on the preservation of faith, the life and dignity of an individual. As such when making healthcare decisions whether to undergo or refuse treatment, the person must first and foremost realize that he is a Muslim who subscribes to Islam , thus he must act in accordance with Islamic teachings. Autonomy in Islam incorporates the interest of not just the individual, but considerations and ethical commitments for the collective as well (S.Khaleefah, 2022). In other words, a Muslim did not have absolute autonomy over his own body since Faith in God means that “ To God we belong. And to Him is our return” (Quran, Al-Imran, 3:109.). This verse establishes that the whole universe belongs to God, and his creation in all forms and sizes will ultimately return to him as the Creator to be judged accordingly.
      • Doctor-patient relationship
      • The duty of the Muslim doctor towards his patient must naturally be in accordance with God’s guidelines. He is but an instrument of God in alleviating people’s illness. When entrusted with the care of a patient, the doctor must offer the needed advice with consideration of the patient’s interest. However, he should never give in to his patient’s demand if they were in violation of God’s orders. He must render whatever assistance to the best of his capability regardless of the financial ability or ethnic origin of the patient. As such, in terms of medical treatment, both the patient and the doctor must take into consideration what is ordained in the Qur’an. As a good Muslim, the patient has to take care of his health, by doing so he is in fact contributing to his family and the community at large.

Its instructions are aimed at the general welfare of all mankind and morality overrides all material benefits that a Muslim stand to gain. It prescribes for a balanced way of life and rejects extremism in living one’s life in both its materialistic and spiritual aspects” (2011). In other words, by taking good care of oneself, he practices the principle of nonmaleficence namely avoiding harm by prevention and treatment of disease and beneficence namely not only for the benefit of the community but also for himself. As such our submission is that the concept of non-maleficence and beneficence in Islam do not only impose a duty on the part of healthcare professionals, in particular doctors, but the duty also rest with patients to avoid harm and in the process gain benefit for themselves.

      If God touch thee with affliction, none can remove it but He; if He touch thee with happiness, He hath power over all things.

In the case of Mohd Hanizam Yunus & anor V Ministry of Health Malaysia [2020], the Court held that the ‘ right to smoke’ is not a fundamental constitutional right and that the smoking ban in public places was a valid public health measure to protect non-smokers.       • Family plays a significant role in medical decision-making. In Malaysia, patients appear to be more inclined towards family members’ involvement in the decision-making process, in particular, when it relates to life and death decision. The patient/wife/deceased passed away during the operation, but the important point is the judgment made by the learned judge on the issue of proxy medical decision where he mentioned that doctors have a duty to not only informed the deceased(wife/patient) about the risks involved but doctors also need to inform the husband. Relational autonomy (JK Walter et.al, 2014) is recognised as part of the social rubric in Malaysian society especially in the Malay Muslim community.
  • Conclusion
    • Islamic principles lay down a pragmatic limitation on the extent of autonomy that are to be allowed, with particular consideration to the foreseeable harms it might cause patients and society, and the probable outcome of the treatment. Regardless of modern medical ethics dilemma that emerged in the wake of technological advancement, Islam provides a general and practical guide that give respect to expert views and scientific evidence for the benefits of mankind. In contrast with the Western medical ethics which predominantly uphold the value of autonomy as of right, Islamic ethics underlines the values of family, society, and public interest which are highly regarded and promoted in Islamic beliefs, and are therefore taken into consideration by the Malaysian Muslim person’s autonomous decisions.

Abstract

In the current era of artificial intelligence (AI), healthcare systems are facing significant challenges, particularly the issue of nursing staff shortages. AI, with its capabilities in efficient data processing, automated decision-making, and machine learning, has been widely applied in the medical field-including medical record analysis, disease prediction, and surgical assistance. These applications help reduce administrative burdens and improve healthcare efficiency. However, AI cannot replace the empathy and interpersonal communication skills of healthcare professionals, and should therefore be viewed as a supportive tool rather than a replacement. The shortage of nursing staff has been exacerbated by factors such as an aging population, declining birth rates, work hour pressures, and inadequate compensation. This shortage negatively impacts patient safety and the quality of care. In response, many countries have begun to introduce foreign healthcare workers and adopt AI-assisted technologies to improve working conditions and address the nursing workforce gap. Despite its potential, the integration of AI into healthcare still faces challenges related to regulations, costs, and ethics. Addressing these issues requires collaborative efforts from governments and various stakeholders to establish clear policies, enhance training and education, and strengthen digital infrastructure. Ultimately, the goal is to achieve a harmonious integration of AI in healthcare that supports sustainable and high-quality care.

    • 人 工 智 慧 已 成 為 現 代 社 會 中 不 可 或 缺 的 一 環 ， 應 用 範 疇 涵 蓋 交 通 、 金 融 、 教 育 與 醫 療 等 多 個 領 域 ， A I 的 核 心 優 勢 包 括 高 效 能 的 資 料 處 理 能 力 、 自 動 化 的 決 策 機 制 ， 以 及 能 夠 透 過 深 度 學 習 持 續 優 化 的 能 力 （Russell & Norvig, 2021） 。 在 醫 療 領 域 功 能 中 ， AI 能 夠 快 速 分 析 大 量 病 歷 資 料 ， 協 助 診 斷 疾 病 預 測 病 況 ， 甚 至 進 行 手 術 機 器 人 的 輔 助 操 作 （Topol, 2019） 。 從 前 瞻 的 角 度 看 AI 所 帶 來 的 科 技 變 革 正 在 重 塑 產 業 架 構 與 勞 動 型 態 ， 提 升 效 率 與 精 確 度 ， 為 社 會 創 造 更 多 的 價 值 ， A I 帶 來 的 便 利 同 時 也 引 發 人 力 被 取 代 的 焦 慮 ， 尤 其 在 高 度 仰 賴 專 業 判 斷 與 人 文 關 懷 的 醫 療 照 護 領 域 ，AI 雖 具 備 龐 大 的 計 算 與 邏 輯 推 理 能 力 ， 卻 難 以 取 代 醫 療 人 員 在 面 對 病 人 時 所 展 現 的 同 理 心 與 溝 通 技 巧 ， 因 此 ， AI 只 能 被 視 為 輔 助 工 具 ， 運 用 及 掌 握 技 術 功 能 ， 共 同 為 創 造 最 佳 的 醫 療 照 護 。
  • 護 理 人 力 不 足 對 醫 療 的 衝 擊 及 影 響
    • 醫 療 體 系 是 社 會 福 祉 的 重 要 基 石 ， 保 障 民 眾 生 命 安 全 與 健 康 權 益 ， 隨 著 人 口 老 化 與 慢 性 病 盛 行 ， 社 會 對 高 品 質 醫 療 服 務 的 需 求 日 益 提 升 （World Health Organization [WHO], 2022） ， 完 善 的 醫 療 照 護 有 助 於 提 升 民 眾 生 活 品 質 ， 更 直 接 影 響 勞 動 力 供 應 與 經 濟 發 展 ， 目 前 民 眾 對 醫 療 的 期 待 已 超 越 基 本 的 疾 病 治 療 ， 轉 為 重 視 預 防 保 健 、 身 心 整 合 與 全 人 照 護 ， 使 得 醫 療 人 員 的 職 責 更 加 複 雜 與 多 元 ， 照 護 需 求 持 續 擴 大 ， 尤 其 在 經 歷 COVID-19 疫 情 肆 虐 下 ， 凸 顯 醫 療 體 系 人 力 資 源 的 可 貴 及 重 要 性 ， 強 化 醫 療 照 護 體 系 與 人 力 資 源 的 穩 定 性 ， 是 當 前 社 會 的 急 迫 任 務 （Liu et al., 2020） 。 護 理 人 員 是 醫 療 體 系 中 佔 有 絕 對 關 鍵 角 色 ， 負 責 執 行 基 礎 照 護 、 協 助 診 療 、 病 人 關 懷 等 多 項 任 務 ， 然 而 ， 隨 著 少 子 化 與 人 口 老 化 的 加 劇 ， 護 理 人 力 短 缺 問 題 日 益 嚴 峻 ， 根 據 衛 福 部 統 計 台 灣 在 2022 年 護 病 比 仍 未 達 理 想 標 準 ， 許 多 醫 院 面 臨 「 一 人 多 工 」 的 窘 境 （ 衛 生 福 利 部 ， 2022） 。 此 外 ， 因 護 理 工 時 過 長 、 壓 力 過 大 及 薪 資 不 符 期 待 等 ， 導 致 護 理 人 員 留 任 意 願 低 ， 職 涯 發 展 受 限 （Chen et al., 2019） 。 護 理 人 力 缺 口 直 接 影 響 整 體 醫 療 品 質 與 病 人 安 全 ， 尤 其 在 急 診 與 加 護 病 房 等 重 症 高 壓 場 域 更 為 明 顯 ， 若 無 法 有 效 留 任 護 理 人 力 ， 不 僅 延 宕 醫 療 流 程 ， 也 將 增 加 現 職 人 員 的 負 荷 形 成 惡 性 循 環 ， 若 持 續 惡 化 下 去 ， 護 理 人 員 流 失 將 削 弱 醫 療 系 統 的 應 變 動 能 ， 逐 漸 影 響 整 體 醫 療 生 態 (Shih et al., 2021) 。
  • 運 用 人 工 智 慧 輔 助 醫 療 人 力
    • 面 對 醫 療 照 護 負 荷 日 益 加 重 與 護 理 人 力 短 缺 的 挑 戰 ， 人 工 智 慧 導 入 提 供 新 的 方 向 ， 然 而 AI 系 統 可 用 於 臨 床 決 策 支 援 、 電 子 病 歷 自 動 化 記 錄 、 健 康 風 險 預 測 等 ， 藉 此 降 低 護 理 人 員 的 行 政 負 擔 與 錯 誤 率 。 例 如 ， 美 國 部 分 醫 院 已 導 入 AI 輔 助 的 預 警 系 統 ， 能 即 時 分 析 病 人 生 命 徵 象 資 料 ， 提 前 辨 識 潛 在 危 機 ， 提 升 照 護 效 率 與 病 人安 全(Esteva et al., 2019) 。 AI 並 非 萬 能 ， 在 醫 療 場 域 中 的 應 用 仍 受 限 於 技 術 成 熟 度 與 倫 理 爭 議 ， 從 人 的 角 度 出 發 ， 醫 療 照 護 並 非 單 純 的 資 訊 處 理 ， 更 涵 蓋 同 理 心 、 文 化 敏 感 性 與 臨 床 判 斷 力 等 「 軟 實 力 」 ， 這 是 AI 短 期 內 無 法 完 全 模 擬 與 取 代 (Morley et al., 2020) 。 因 此 ， 未 來 醫 療 體 系 應 朝 向 人 與 AI 協 作 的 模 式 發 展 ， 強 化 醫 療 人 員 的 數 位 素 養 ， 並 透 過 AI 技 術 釋 放 人 力 資 源 ， 讓 醫 護 專 業 人 員 能 更 專 注 於 照 護 核 心 工 作 。
  • 實 際 案 例 分 析 與 比 較 - 以 國 際 護 理 缺 工 對 策 為 借 鏡
    • 全 球 多 國 皆 面 臨 護 理 人 力 不 足 的 困 境 ， 但 因 應 策 略 與 成 效 不 一 。 以 日 本 為 例 ， 該 國 同 樣 因 高 齡 化 加 劇 導 致 照 護 需 求 急 增 ， 政 府 遂 積 極 推 動 引 進 外 籍 看 護 實 習 生 ， 並 搭 配 A I 機 器 人 輔 助 長 照 機 構 作 業 ， 以 降 低 照 護 人 力 壓 力 (Tsukada & Tokoro, 2020) ； 反 觀 德 國 ， 則 透 過 提 升 護 理 薪 資 與 改 善 職 場 環 境 吸 引 本 地 年 輕 人 投 入 ， 成 效 逐 步 顯 現 (Kuhlmann et al., 2021) 。 台 灣 則 仍 以 醫 院 為 中 心 的 醫 療 照 護 模 式 為 主 ， 對 護 理 人 員 的 依 賴 度 高 ， 但 相 關 政 策 支 援 與 制 度 彈 性 仍 待 加 強 ， 在 比 較 各 國 對 策 後 可 見 ， 只 有 靠 技 術 或 人 力 引 進 難 以 根 本 解 決 問 題 ， 唯 有 結 合 職 場 改 革 、 教 育 訓 練 與 A I 應 用 ， 才 能 建 立 永 續 的 醫 療 人 力 體 系 。
  • AI 導 入 的 困 難 與 挑 戰 法 規 、 成 本 與 倫 理 爭 議
    • 儘 管 AI 在 醫 療 領 域 展 現 潛 力 ， 實 際 導 入 過 程 仍 面 臨 諸 多 挑 戰 。 首 先 ， 醫 療 AI 的 部 署 需 符 合 嚴 格 法 規 規 範 與 數 據 隱 私 保 護 標 準 ， 如 歐 盟 GDPR 或 台 灣 個 資 法 ， 否 則 恐 涉 法 律 風 險 (Gerke et al., 2020) 。 其 次 ， 高 昂 的 建 置 與 維 護 成 本 對 資 源 有 限 的 中 小 型 醫 療 機 構 而 言 ， 是 一 大 門 檻 ， 病 人 與 醫 療 人 員 對 AI 系 統 的 信 任 度 亦 影 響 其 接 受 程 度 與 實 際 應 用 範 圍 ， 倫 理 議 題 亦 不 容 忽 視 ， AI 是 否 能 在 危 急 情 境 中 做 出 適 當 決 策 、 如 何 避 免 演 算 法 錯 誤 等 ， 時 間 可 以 被 期 待 證 明 。 因 此 ， 政 府 與 醫 療 機 構 在 推 動 AI 應 用 時 ， 應 建 立 跨 專 業 合 作 平 台 ， 邀 請 資 訊 、 醫 療 、 法 律 與 倫 理 專 家 共 同 參 與 ， 制 定 明 確 規 範 與 標 準 ， 方 能 健 全 AI 在 醫 療 體 系 中 的 發 展 。 為 回 應 AI 時 代 下 醫 療 照 護 負 荷 與 護 理 缺 工 的 雙 重 挑 戰 ， 政 策 層 面 必 須 從 人 力 資 源 、 教 育 訓 練 、 數 位 基 礎 與 倫 理 法 規 等 多 方 面 整 合 推 進 ， 首 先 ， 政 府 應 透 過 提 升 護 理 待 遇 、 改 善 工 時 與 建 立 職 涯 支 持 系 統 ， 強 化 護 理 專 業 的 吸 引 力 與 留 任 率 (Buchan et al., 2022) 。 應 納 入 AI 素 養 與 跨 領 域 知 識 於 護 理 教 育 體 系 ， 使 新 世 代 護 理 人 員 能 有 效 應 用 數 位 工 具 提 升 照 護 品 質 ， 在 技 術 層 面 ， 應 積 極 推 動 智 慧 醫 療 系 統 建 設 ， 鼓 勵 醫 療 機 構 採 用 AI 輔 助 系 統 ， 並 提 供 補 助 資 源 與 技 術 指 導 。 法 規 部 分 也 需 要 儘 速 訂 定 AI 應 用 之 倫 理 準 則 與 資 料 安 全 法 規 ， 建 立 信 任 基 礎 ， 最 終 目 標 為 建 構 「 人 機 協 作 」 的 新 型 醫 療 生 態 系 ， 讓 AI 輔 助 重 複 性 與 高 負 荷 的 工 作 ， 醫 護 人 員 則 專 注 於 關 鍵 照 護 與 人 際 互 動 ， 達 成 效 率 與 人 性 的 平 衡 。
  • 結 論
    • 「 科 技 始 終 來 自 於 人 性 」 這 是 Nokia 很 有 名 的 一 句 廣 告 slogan 原 文 是 「 Connecting People 」 ， 很 多 創 新 都 來 自 於 現 實 需 求 ， 而 現 今 時 代 AI 時 代 帶 來 醫 療 創 新 契 機 ， 也 同 時 凸 顯 傳 統 照 護 模 式 的 限 制 與 挑 戰 。 護 理 缺 工 作 為 醫 療 人 力 短 缺 的 縮 影 ， 不 僅 衝 擊 醫 療 品 質 ， 也 影 響 整 體 公 共 衛 生 與 社 會 穩 定 。 透 過 AI 技 術 的 導 入 ， 固 然 可 在 一 定 程 度 上 緩 解 人 力 壓 力 ， 但 唯 有 在 完 善 政 策 、 教 育 與 倫 理 架 構 的 支 撐 下 ， 才 能 真 正 發 揮 其 價 值 。 面 對 未 來 ， 應 從 制 度 面 強 化 護 理 職 場 環 境 、 培 育 數 位 人 才 ， 並 積 極 建 構 人 與 機 器 共 同 協 作 的 照 護 文 化 。 唯 有 如 此 ， 才 能 在 科 技 快 速 演 進 的 浪 潮 中 ， 打 造 一 個 兼 顧 效 率 、 同 理 與 永 續 的 醫 療 照 護 體 系 ， 實 現 醫 療 照 護 與 人 工 智 慧 結 合 及 輔 助 永 續 照 護 的 共 榮 時 代 。
